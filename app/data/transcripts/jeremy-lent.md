## Abstract

Jeremy Lent asks what we are trying to save when we speak of saving civilization. He argues that the present system is not broken. It does what it was built to do: extract wealth from nature and from most people, and pass it to a few. He traces this to a worldview of separation that arose in early modern Europe, and he shows its results in the Great Acceleration and the overshoot of the planetary boundaries.

He offers a different foundation. Drawing on systems science and on traditional wisdom, he says that interconnection and cooperation are our nature. From this he builds the idea of an ecocivilization, a civilization based on life. Its principles include the intrinsic value of all life, human dignity, diversity, subsidiarity, structural equity and design for cooperation. He describes what these could mean for the measure of success, the commons, universal basic income, corporate charters, citizens' assemblies, planetary justice and the rights of nature. He closes with islands of coherence, the three horizons of change and the Symbioscene. He invites everyone to take part, because the unravelling of the old system opens room to reweave society around life.

In the dialogue, Mark van Wyk speaks of despair and an ember of hope. John Varney asks how people change. Nina asks for more urgency and describes agroecology in South Africa. Michael Stock, William Frater and Klaus Mager raise the future, the legal system and a water-short county in Oregon. Glen T. Martin describes the Earth Constitution. Peter Anderson invites a follow-up on operationalizing an ecocivilization. Jeremy answers each with the same thread: people and systems change one another, and the work is to remind people who they are.
