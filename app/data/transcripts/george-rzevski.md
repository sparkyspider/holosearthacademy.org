## Abstract

Professor George Rzevski, speaking at the age of 93, offers complexity science as a bridge between holism and artificial intelligence, and between what C. P. Snow called the "two cultures" of the humanities and the sciences. He sorts all systems by their degree of uncertainty. Deterministic systems, such as clocks, cars and algorithms, are all man-made, and nature offers no example of one. Complex systems, such as ecosystems, markets, the human brain and the digital brain, are open and self-organising. They hold knowledge, have no central control, and show emergent properties that cannot be found in any of their parts.

He then traces the co-evolution of society and technology, from James Watt's steam engine in 1775 to Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web in 1989. The key resource moves from land to capital to AI, and at each switch complexity rises. Human intelligence remains superior in holistic work: philosophical, strategic, creative, caring. AI is an order of magnitude more productive in about half of today's jobs, and costs almost nothing to run once commissioned. Comparing the human brain with the digital brain, he finds little reason to fear AI. He proposes that the wealth AI creates fund education, health, housing, food and water. Human evolution, through holistic education, should be our focus, rather than blocking AI.

The conversation that follows ranges widely. Klaus Mager describes agents built around collaborative hybrid intelligence for food systems. Marc Pierson asks which problems belong at which scale, citing Ashby's Law. Dr Rod Kersh and Rzevski discuss the healer's touch and AI in medicine. Christopher Cooke, Berry Behr and Michael Stock raise human sensitivity, indigenous conversation with the land, and the loss of jobs. Rzevski answers that no map of the future can exist, because the future self-organises. Claudius van Wyk closes with the idea of presencing.

### Rzevski — the language of complexity, information and Plato

**George Rzevski:** I observe how you use the language of complexity science when you talk. Self-organisation comes very naturally here and there. That was not taken up later by philosophers; he was just labelled an idealist [captioned "ideologists"; ?].
