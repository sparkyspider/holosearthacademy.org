## Abstract

Llewellyn van Wyk reads Jan Smuts as an early theorist of the constructed world. He opens with Ezra Pound's lines from 1912: "we have pulled down the stars to our will." They capture an age of confident technological mastery. The built environment, he argues, is now the main interface between human civilization and planetary systems. After the Second World War, the CSIR was charged with building technologies for a displaced world. Göbekli Tepe, raised by hunter-gatherers around 9600 BCE, shows building beginning as an instrument of collective meaning.

Modern construction treats buildings as isolated objects. He proposes evolving wholes instead, each building a personality with its own field of effects. The circular economy still sees buildings as recoverable parts, so recycling becomes regression. He calls for structural permanence, adaptive reuse and reversible tectonics. A wagon workshop in Philadelphia became a mill, then an art gallery and restaurant. Architects become stewards of systems, and AI can serve whole-systems judgment if we choose. Section 24 of the South African Constitution binds human rights to ecological ones. The earthen settlements of the Sahel and Seattle's Bullitt Center maintain wholes across time. NEOM's The Line is a mechanistic whole. Portland's Lloyd Crossing asks what its site was before settlement.

Holism, he concludes, asks us to build differently, so that what we make takes part in evolution. In the dialogue, Mark van Wyk asks whether homes should last a thousand years or biodegrade. The answer is flexibility and adaptability. Michael Stock raises Hundertwasser, the Māori and Christchurch, which leads to New Zealand's debate about retreat from failing coasts. Richard Cox, Jeff Blumberg, George Rzevski, Joshua Malkin and Egon Hus respond. Llewellyn van Wyk closes with the shift from the circle to the square, the Neolithic rupture we still live in.
