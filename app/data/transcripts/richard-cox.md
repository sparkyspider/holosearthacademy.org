## Abstract

Richard Cox builds his talk on one sentence from Jan Smuts. A living whole is explained by its future as well as by its past and present. Cox asks us to carry two phrases through the evening: the seeds of the future and the pull of the future. Organic farming and thirty years of Waldorf teaching led him to the resonance between Goethe and Smuts. That resonance became his book *Signs and Signatures*. Reading nature, he says, is like reading between the lines. It asks for careful attention to gestures and polarities. Radiating meets enclosing in the pine and the acacia. Movement meets rest and gives rise to rhythm, in flags and waves.

Cox then turns to living forms. Goethe saw leaves change into petals and linked this to Proteus. The young and the adult chimpanzee suggest that the human form holds specialization back. Smuts called this restraint. The skull, chest and limbs echo the polarities of rest, rhythm and movement, and thinking, feeling and willing sit with them. Wolfgang Schad's work shows rodents, carnivores and ungulates repeating a threefold pattern in temperament, teeth and jaw movement. Embryos, the human hand and the white of the eye show an unspecialized human form. A long childhood in the care of mothers keeps that openness alive.

Cox closes with education. In his school he kept learning broad and kept every potential open, so that thinking, feeling and willing all grow. Premature specialization can close possibilities. Teachers must stay open to a world that is calling. In the dialogue, Rod Kersh asks about specialists, and Cox replies that the concern is premature specialization. Jeff Blumberg, Glen T. Martin, Marlene De Lange and Marcus Link connect the talk to nature's patterns, governance, personality and the two hemispheres of the brain.
